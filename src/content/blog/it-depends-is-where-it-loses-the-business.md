I've answered an executive's yes-or-no question with "it depends" and watched the room cool by a degree. Not because the answer was wrong — it was the most accurate thing I could have said — but because of what the people across the table heard. They asked for a direction. I handed the decision back to them with a caveat stapled to it. To me that was rigor. To them it sounded like I didn't want to be pinned down.

"It depends" is true almost every time the business asks IT a clean yes-or-no question. It is also, said reflexively, one of the most expensive habits in the profession. The honesty behind it is real — the answer genuinely is conditional, and a confident yes or no would be a lie. But honesty about uncertainty and unwillingness to commit look identical from the other side of the table. The business hears a hedge; you meant a caveat. That gap is where credibility leaks out, one meeting at a time.

---

## What the room actually hears

When an executive asks "will this project be done by the end of the quarter?" or "is this vendor a security risk?", they are not asking you to narrate the problem space. They are collecting an input for a decision they have to make and own. "It depends" returns that input unprocessed. From their chair, the conditional-first answer is indistinguishable from someone buying time, covering themselves, or quietly admitting they don't know.

The asymmetry is the whole problem. IT optimizes for being technically correct — and "it depends" is technically correct. The business is grading for something else entirely: usefulness under uncertainty. Those are different tests, and the answer that aces the first one can fail the second. An engineer in the room hears "it depends" and nods, because the answer really is conditional and they know it. The CFO in the same room hears it and writes you down, just slightly, as a person who couldn't give them what they came for.

Both reactions are correct. That's what makes the habit so hard to see from the inside.

---

## The two fears it's protecting

Pull the hedge apart and you usually find two fears underneath it. The first is being wrong in front of leadership — committing to "yes, by the quarter" and missing. The second is being quoted back to yourself later, stripped of context: *but you said it was fine.* Anyone who has been on the receiving end of either knows they are not imaginary.

Here is the uncomfortable part. "It depends" doesn't actually protect you from either one. It defers the moment of commitment, but the commitment still comes due — and in the meantime you've traded a small, recoverable accuracy risk for a steady, compounding credibility tax. Being occasionally wrong and visibly accountable is survivable. Being reliably evasive is the thing that gets IT left out of the room where the decisions are made.

---

## Answer first, condition second

The fix is structural, and it is not the same as pretending to be certain. The move is to lead with a real answer and put the caveat in its proper, subordinate place. "Yes — with one condition that could change it." "Probably not, and here's the single thing that would flip it." "On track, assuming the vendor hits the date they've committed to — if they slip, so do we."

Notice what survived. The uncertainty is still there, stated plainly. What changed is the order. You committed to a direction, then exposed the hinge the direction turns on. The executive walks away with something to act on *and* the honest condition attached to it — in that sequence. Same information as "it depends." Opposite credibility outcome.

The reordering also does something quieter: it tells the business where to look. "It depends" leaves them to guess what it depends *on*. "Yes, unless the vendor slips" hands them the one variable that matters and, implicitly, the thing to go manage. You've turned a hedge into a heading.

---

## When "it depends" is the right answer

Sometimes the honest answer really is "two paths, and the choice is yours, not mine." Regulatory risk tolerance, build-versus-buy, how much downtime the business is willing to underwrite — these are genuinely the business's calls, and pretending otherwise is its own failure of translation. So the goal is not to abolish "it depends." It's to never leave it un-framed.

A framed "it depends" lays out the branches: here are the two paths, here's what each one costs, here's the one I'd take and why. That is not a punt — that is precisely the value an IT leader is supposed to add. The un-framed version drops the fork in the road on the table and walks away; the framed version walks the executive to the fork, describes both roads, and points. The difference between those two is the difference between sounding like an obstacle and sounding like a partner.

---

## The commitment is the translation

Underneath all of this is a thing the business already knows about IT and rarely says out loud: it does not need you to be right about everything. It needs you to be legible and accountable. Committing to a direction — and owning the revision out loud when the conditions move — builds more trust than perfect-but-uncommitted accuracy ever will. The caveat was never the problem. The order was.

So the next time the reflex rises and "it depends" is halfway out, hold it for one beat and reach for the version that leads. Give the answer. Then expose the hinge.

Not "it depends." "Here's my answer — and here's exactly what would change it."
